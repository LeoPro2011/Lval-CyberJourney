## 02/10/2026 — Networking Concepts

**Focus:** Networking fundamentals  
**Objective:** Build a solid understanding of networking and network analysis before moving toward network pentesting.

### What I worked on

Today I started the **Networking** module on TryHackMe and began building my networking foundations.

I started with the **OSI model** and learned its seven layers. This gave me a first framework for understanding how network communication is structured.

I then completed the **TCP/IP model** section of the TryHackMe **Networking Concepts** room.

### OSI and TCP/IP

I learned that the OSI and TCP/IP models use different ways of grouping network functions. Three layers correspond closely between the models, while the TCP/IP model groups several OSI functions differently, including its **Internet layer**.

One important takeaway was that these models are conceptual frameworks rather than descriptions of different physical devices. The same communication can be viewed through different abstraction models.

I found the TCP/IP model appealing because it keeps the essential concepts without the additional granularity of the OSI model.

### IP addresses and subnetting

I started learning about **IPv4 addressing and subnetting**.

One important correction to my initial understanding is that it is not simply that the octets 0 and 255 are universally reserved. In a typical IPv4 subnet, the **network address** and **broadcast address** occupy the first and last address of that particular subnet respectively. Their exact values depend on the subnet mask.

I also learned that IPv4 provides just under **4.3 billion possible 32-bit addresses** in the theoretical address space, although the number of globally usable public IPv4 addresses is substantially smaller because of reserved, private and special-purpose ranges.

### Encapsulation

I discovered **encapsulation**: as data moves down the networking stack, each layer adds information needed by the corresponding protocol. This helped me understand how the different networking layers work together rather than seeing protocols as isolated technologies.

### TCP and UDP

I learned the fundamental difference between **TCP and UDP**.

- **TCP** is connection-oriented and provides reliable, ordered delivery with mechanisms such as retransmission and flow/congestion control.
- **UDP** is connectionless and provides a lightweight datagram transport without TCP's built-in reliability and ordering guarantees.

My initial description of this as "communicating versus simply sending data" was too simplistic. Both are used to send data; the important distinction is the transport behaviour and guarantees required by the application.

## 03/10/2026 — Network Services

**Focus:** Network-service enumeration and exploitation in an authorized TryHackMe lab.

Today I continued the networking track with TryHackMe's **Network Services** room. The room covered SMB, Telnet and FTP, with an emphasis on enumeration and exploiting intentionally vulnerable services in an authorized lab.

### SMB

I learned that **SMB (Server Message Block)** is a network protocol used primarily in Windows environments for sharing resources such as files and printers over TCP/IP.

I practiced SMB enumeration with:

- `enum4linux -a`
- `smbclient`

Enumeration exposed information including the target OS version and available shares. One interesting share was `profiles`.

I connected to the share with `smbclient` and, because the lab server allowed anonymous access, I was able to enumerate and download files without normal credentials. Inside the share I found a `.ssh` directory and an `id_rsa` private key associated with the lab user **cactus**.

I downloaded the key, corrected its local permissions, and used it to authenticate to SSH as `cactus`. The user's files contained the room's flag.

**Key lesson:** a poorly configured SMB service can expose sensitive shares or files. Anonymous access and excessive share permissions are therefore important enumeration targets during an authorized assessment.

### Telnet and the backdoor lab

The next section introduced Telnet. I had already experimented with Telnet during the previous day's Networking Concepts room.

I started with Nmap enumeration. Because the usual ports were not enough to identify the service, I scanned the complete TCP port range with:

`-p1-65535`

The open service was identified as **Skidy's Backdoor**. I then connected through Telnet and worked through the lab's intended exploitation chain to obtain the final flag, using Netcat as the listener and the lab-provided payload-generation workflow.

I also fixed my OpenVPN setup during the exercise after noticing that two configuration/process files were running simultaneously.

### FTP

I then moved to **FTP (File Transfer Protocol)**.

I learned that FTP separates communication into a **control channel** and a **data channel**, and that active and passive FTP differ in which side initiates the data connection.

For the lab, I enumerated a target with TCP port 21 open and identified **vsftpd**.

I connected using FTP anonymous access and found a file containing a username candidate. I then tested the credentials in the lab and successfully authenticated as **mike** with the intentionally weak password **password**. This gave access to `ftp.txt`, which contained the flag.

**Key lesson:** an exposed FTP service can become a serious security issue when anonymous access, weak credentials or unnecessary file exposure are enabled.

### Cisco — Introduction to Cybersecurity, Module 4

After the TryHackMe session, I continued with Cisco Networking Academy's **Introduction to Cybersecurity** course and completed **Module 4 — Protecting the Enterprise**.

This section was more theoretical than the TryHackMe work and focused on the foundations of defensive security, including both technical and behavioural/physical aspects of protecting an organization.

### Defensive security concepts

I reviewed the role of common network-security equipment and concepts, including **firewalls, routers and switches**. I also learned about **NetFlow**, which can be used to collect and analyse information about network traffic flows, and about **honeypots** as a defensive/deception mechanism that can help security teams observe attacker behaviour.

### Basic penetration-testing methodology

The course introduced the main stages of a basic penetration test:

1. **Planning** — determining the scope and where passive or active reconnaissance and vulnerability research will be performed.
2. **Analysis / scanning** — identifying systems, services and potential attack paths. I connected this concept with tools such as Nmap, although the course section itself is primarily methodological.
3. **Gaining access** — exploiting vulnerabilities, weak controls, social-engineering opportunities or physical-security weaknesses within the authorized scope.
4. **Maintaining access** — understanding how an attacker could preserve access, for example through malware such as a Trojan.
5. **Reporting** — translating technical findings into clear language that an organization can understand and act upon.

The course then covered how an organization should respond to a penetration-test report: communicate problems honestly and responsibly, identify the precise root causes, apply lessons learned and verify the effectiveness of remediation repeatedly.

### Risk management

I learned the basic risk-management cycle:

- identify threats and their potential effects;
- evaluate their severity;
- decide how to respond in order to eliminate or reduce the risk;
- monitor the risk continuously so that recurring or related problems can be detected.

Cisco presented threats broadly, including attacks, process or product failures, service interruptions, reputational damage, legal exposure and loss of intellectual property.

### IDS, IPS, DLP and SIEM

I also learned the basic roles of several defensive technologies:

- **IDS (Intrusion Detection System):** monitors activity for indicators of malicious or suspicious traffic and generates alerts.
- **IPS (Intrusion Prevention System):** can actively block or reject traffic when it matches configured rules or attack signatures.
- **DLP (Data Loss Prevention):** helps prevent sensitive information from being leaked or lost.
- **SIEM (Security Information and Event Management):** collects and analyses security logs, alerts and other telemetry from multiple sources to support monitoring and investigation.

The course also introduced **Cisco ISE** and **TrustSec** as examples of Cisco technologies used to bring identity, access control and network-security information together. I am treating these as concepts I have encountered rather than technologies I can currently administer.
